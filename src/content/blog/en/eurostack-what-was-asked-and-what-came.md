In my article on [Tapestry](/blog/is-tapestry-the-future-of-sovereign-ai), I used EuroStack as the
frame for what Europe would have to bring to sovereign AI: a model is only the top layer of a
stack that Europe mostly does not control. But which EuroStack? The name now covers a research
report, an industry coalition and a few other initiatives, which do not ask for the same things.

This article separates them, then compares what they asked for with what the European Commission
proposed on 3 June 2026 in its European Technological Sovereignty Package. Everything below comes
from the published texts and press coverage, as of the end of October 2026.

## One name, several projects

The idea was launched at a conference in the European Parliament on 24 September 2024, "Towards
European Digital Independence: Building the EuroStack". Two main projects grew out of it.

**The report.** [EuroStack – A European Alternative for Digital Sovereignty](https://www.euro-stack.info/)
was published by the Bertelsmann Stiftung in February 2025. Its lead authors are Francesca Bria,
Paul Timmers and Fausto Gernone, with support from CEPS, Stiftung Mercator and UCL's Institute for
Innovation and Public Purpose. It is 128 pages long and released under CC BY 4.0.

**The industry initiative.** In January 2025, the economist Cristina Caffarra drafted a pitch
paper for an industry-led EuroStack. On 14 March 2025, an
[open letter](https://eurostack.eu/the-letter/) to Ursula von der Leyen and Executive
Vice-President Henna Virkkunen followed, signed at launch by around a hundred organisations, among
them Airbus, Dassault Systèmes, OVHcloud, Nextcloud and Proton. The group became the EuroStack
Initiative Foundation in October 2025, and its site now lists nearly 500 supporters.

The two are related: Caffarra is listed among the report's contributors, and the letter cites the
report in its first footnote. A third body, the European DIGITAL SME Alliance, runs its own
EuroStack programme, and other sites catalogue "EuroStack" alternatives. When someone says
EuroStack, it is worth asking which one.

## The report: seven layers and a public mission

The report's main contribution is a picture. It describes the digital stack as seven layers, from
the bottom up:

1. Resources: critical raw materials, energy, water and skilled people
2. Chips
3. Networks
4. Connected devices and IoT
5. Cloud infrastructure
6. Software platforms, applications and algorithms
7. Data and artificial intelligence

Cybersecurity and emerging technologies run across all of them. The report estimates that over
80% of Europe's digital infrastructure and technologies are imported.

What I find useful is less the list than the discipline it imposes. Every sovereignty claim can be
asked "on which layer?" A European model trained on American GPUs, in a European data centre,
under a European licence, is sovereign on one layer and dependent on two others. That was the
point of my Tapestry article, and it applies to almost every "sovereign" offer I see.

The report then proposes a programme:

- **Money**: a target of €300 billion in investment over ten years, starting with a €10 billion
  European Sovereign Tech Fund to finance shared building blocks through an open competition.
- **Procurement**: a "Europe-first, comply-or-explain" approach, with 50% of public cloud spending
  going to EU-based providers by 2030.
- **Open source**: an "open-source first" policy, promoting and, where necessary, mandating open
  source for public services and critical infrastructure.
- **Governance**: a EuroStack steering committee and a Chief EuroStack Officer, with civil society
  at the table.

Its seven principles include "de-proprietarization" and "data as a common good". The report is
industrial policy, but its stated purpose is public interest.

## The coalition: buy European

The industry letter shares the diagnosis, and adds urgency: "our reliance on non-European
technologies will become almost complete in less than three years at current rates." Its
priorities are different, though. The signatories ask for:

- a formal requirement for the public sector to "Buy European", meaning "European-led and
  assembled solutions", while accepting that supply chains include non-European parts;
- harmonised sovereign-cloud certification, with a higher bar than the draft EUCS scheme;
- a Sovereign Infrastructure Fund, underwritten by the European Investment Bank and national
  funding bodies, for the capital-heavy layers;
- an "industry-led effort", with the Commission convening working groups with industry.

Open source appears, as one way to build supply and interoperability. It is not the founding
principle it is in the report. Put simply, the report asks "who governs the stack?" and the
coalition asks "who sells it?". Both answers matter, and they are not the same. A public
administration that replaces a US SaaS with a European proprietary one has moved jurisdiction, but
its dependency on a single vendor is unchanged.

## What the Commission proposed in June 2026

The [European Technological Sovereignty Package](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_26_1187)
contains four items:

1. **Chips Act 2.0**, a legislative proposal focused on permits, demand and strategic projects.
2. **The Cloud and AI Development Act (CADA)**, COM(2026) 502, a legislative proposal.
3. **A Communication on European tech sovereignty**, with an **EU Open Source Strategy**,
   COM(2026) 503.
4. **A roadmap for digitalisation and AI in the energy sector**.

The Digital Networks Act, proposed in January, and the AI Gigafactories, whose call opened in
July, are separate.

The package has real content. CADA aims to triple EU data-centre capacity in five to seven years,
creates a sovereignty framework for cloud services with four assurance levels, and sets up a
voluntary EuroCloud Federation for the public sector. The open source strategy adds an EU
catalogue, a network of open source programme offices, a target of 30 million users of open
source collaboration tools by 2030, and an Open Source Maintenance Instrument whose stated aim
includes "creating a European capacity and capability to fork projects". That last measure is one
of the most concrete answers to a dependency almost every public system shares: critical
components maintained by a handful of volunteers, or by a single company that can change the
licence.

## What it left out

Put next to the report, the gaps are clear.

| EuroStack report                                           | Tech Sovereignty Package                                                                                         |
| ---------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| €10 bn Sovereign Tech Fund, now                            | No new fund; a future European Competitiveness Fund in the 2028–2034 budget                                       |
| €300 bn over ten years                                     | Investment needs of over €400 bn, mostly expected from the private sector                                         |
| Europe-first procurement, 50% of public cloud spend by 2030 | Sovereignty levels set by national risk assessments; European contribution as an "ancillary and not decisive" criterion |
| Open source mandated for public services                   | Member States must "encourage" open source; guidelines                                                            |
| A EuroStack governance body                                | Existing bodies and national authorities                                                                          |

**Money.** The euro figures in the Communication are estimates of what must be mobilised:
€120 billion for chips, around €200 billion for data centres by 2036, €100 billion for cloud, AI
and gigafactories, and €2 billion for open source over seven years. None of it is a new budget
line. The Commission also announced a consultation on "European equity capacity at scale". As
The Parliament Magazine put it, the package "did not clarify where the money would come from".

**Buying European.** CADA's procurement article is more careful than either EuroStack asked for.
All public buyers need cloud services recognised at the lowest level or above. Those in NIS2
sectors and in areas such as security, defence and justice must buy at level 2 or higher, but each
Member State runs its own risk assessment, and exceptions apply when no recognised service exists
or the cost is disproportionate. For innovative cloud and AI purchases, buyers must include
criteria on the contribution to a European ecosystem, but these must be "ancillary and not
decisive". Caffarra called the buy-European provision "very feeble" in the press, and analysts
quoted alongside her noted that most cloud contracts would remain open to all providers.

**Open source first.** The CADA article is titled "open source first", but it asks Member States
to encourage public bodies to use open source, taking cost and security into account. The strategy
says an open source first principle in public procurement "could" reverse the trend, and proposes
guidelines. The report asked for a mandate by 2026.

**Governance.** There is no EuroStack body and no single owner of the stack as a whole.
Implementation runs through national authorities and the existing European Digital Infrastructure
Consortia, including the Digital Commons EDIC created by France, Germany, Italy and the
Netherlands in October 2025. The word EuroStack appears once, in a footnote.

## What it means for a public buyer today

CADA is still a proposal. The Parliament appointed its rapporteurs in June, and no committee vote
has taken place yet; the co-rapporteur hopes to conclude negotiations by the end of 2027. Most of
the systems I work on will be re-tendered before it applies. So I would not wait for it.

- **Classify by layer and by system.** Use the seven layers to find where your real dependencies
  sit, and the Commission's [Cloud Sovereignty Framework](/blog/eu-cloud-sovereignty-framework)
  to set a minimum level per system. The CADA levels will not map one to one onto it, but the
  questions are the same.
- **Prefer what you can leave.** A European vendor you cannot leave is a smaller risk than an
  American one you cannot leave, but it is still a risk. Open standards, open source and tested exit
  plans are what keep the next choice open.
- **Budget for maintenance, not only for licences.** If the Open Source Maintenance Instrument
  arrives, it will help. Until then, the components you depend on most are maintained by people
  you probably do not pay.

## My reading

The package moves in the direction both EuroStacks asked for, and it adopts some of their
vocabulary: sovereignty levels, open source first, a federation of public clouds. It does not
adopt their two central demands, money now and a procurement preference with teeth. The report's
most distinctive idea, that the stack should be governed as a public good and not only bought from
European companies, has the weakest echo of all.

That leaves the decisions where they were: with each Member State, and with each public buyer
writing a tender. In my experience, that is where sovereignty is actually won or lost, one
contract and one architecture at a time.

What I will watch in 2027:

- the CADA committee vote, and whether Article 32 gains weight or loses it;
- the first national risk assessments under CADA, and how many contracts they put at level 2 or
  higher;
- whether the Open Source Maintenance Instrument gets a budget before 2028;
- the AI Gigafactory selections, and whether any capacity is reserved for open, multi-country
  models.
